# Literature and practice review for predictive autoscaling

**Date:** 2026-09-22 · **Scope:** what published systems and forecasting research say that should change this
autoscaler's design (per-pod request rate, 10-minute resolution, six steps to 60 minutes, 24-hour input window, 6-hourly
retraining on 7 rolling days, neural forecast blended with a same-time-previous-days pattern, operator taking
`max(forecast, reactive, min)` with an overestimate cap, scale-down stabilisation and reactive fallback).

Written against the measured position in [`eval/RESULTS-2026-09-22-corrected.md`](../eval/RESULTS-2026-09-22-corrected.md):
the neural arm has not beaten the strongest arithmetic baseline on any of ten runs, the best baseline flips between the
seasonal pattern (steady traffic) and the trend-adaptive rule (trend, level shift, weekly), and across-seed instability
is unexplained. Nothing below contradicts that result. **Nothing below independently validates it either:** every paper
cited here is evidence about other systems, other traces and other horizons, and none of it excuses a defect in this
implementation.

## 1. What published predictive autoscalers actually do

**The production systems are conservative, and the largest of them contains no sequence model at all.** Google's
Autopilot [1] drives Borg limits from exponentially-decayed usage histograms (half-life 12 h CPU, 48 h memory), taking a
percentile of a *load-adjusted* histogram (95th/90th for serving jobs, 98th or peak for memory), adding a 10–15 % safety
margin, then taking the maximum recommendation over the last hour. Its "machine learning" recommender is an ensemble of
`argmin` models differing only in decay rate and safety margin, each minimising
`w_o·overrun + w_u·underrun + w_ΔL·(limit changed)`; the recommender serves whichever model has the lowest
exponentially-smoothed historical cost, with explicit penalties for switching model or limit (Eq. 8–9). Reported:
an average relative slack of 23 % for Autopiloted jobs on the ML recommender against 46 % for manually-managed jobs on
soft limits (60 % on hard limits), and a 10× reduction in jobs severely affected by OOM.

**[Corrected 2026-09-22]** Autopiloted jobs on the *moving-window* recommenders average 31 % slack, and an earlier
draft of this review read the 31 % → 23 % difference as "the model-selection layer alone is worth ~8 slack points".
**That attribution is withdrawn.** Section 4.2 reports the slack distribution of *different observational cohorts of
jobs* — whichever recommender each job happened to be using — not a controlled selector-only ablation; the paper
itself cautions that "these comparisons might be biased" and shows that Autopiloted jobs have smaller footprints.
Nor are Autopilot's candidates forecasts: both families are parameterised **vertical resource-limit** recommenders
(§3.2.3, an arg-min over histogram bucket bounds parameterised by decay rate and safety margin), while the candidates
this project would select among are demand forecasts. What survives, and is worth copying, is the **mechanism**, not
the number: choose among candidates by exponentially-smoothed realised cost, with explicit penalties for switching
candidate and for changing the setting (Eq. 8 and Eq. 9). How much that mechanism is worth *here* is unknown until
measured here. Microsoft's Resource Central [38] is the same shape as Autopilot overall: behaviour learned offline
from history, served online to resource managers.

**The cloud vendors' predictive scaling is scale-out-only, coarse, and shipped with a shadow mode.** AWS EC2 predictive
scaling needs ≥24 h of data, analyses up to 14 days, emits an **hourly** forecast for the next 48 h refreshed **every
6 hours**, never scales in, launches early via `SchedulingBufferTime`, and combines with dynamic policies by taking the
**maximum** desired capacity across policies [2]. Azure's predictive autoscale forecasts CPU up to 60 minutes ahead from
7–15 days of history, is scale-out only, and ships a "forecast only" mode [3]. Netflix's Scryer [4] keyed on day-of-week
periodicity and ran alongside reactive scaling rather than replacing it. The additive `max(forecast, reactive, min)`
rule here is the rule AWS applies across policies, layered over an HPA-style reactive floor [37]: mainstream and well
supported.

**Microsoft's production serverless policy is a histogram with rules-based fallback.** "Serverless in the Wild" [5]
keeps a per-application histogram of inter-invocation times, falls back to a fixed keep-alive when data is thin or the
histogram is unrepresentative, and uses ARIMA *only* for applications whose intervals exceed the histogram range.

**Academic and industrial Kubernetes/cloud work, and what it claims.** Alibaba's AHPA [6] forecasts with robust seasonal
decomposition, learns a workload→pod mapping, and shifts the forecast forward to cover pod start-up (~10 % higher CPU
usage, >20 % cost reduction; deployed since 2021). MagicScaler [7] (VLDB 2023) pairs multi-scale attention with a
Gaussian process and feeds the *distribution* into a receding-horizon minimisation of idle, scale-out and draining cost:
QoS loss falls to 11.7 % of a purely reactive policy at +1.2 % cost — but to 4.9 % of a "Statistics" baseline (past-week
statistics plus buffer) at **+43.9 %** cost. OptScaler [8] (Alipay/VLDB 2024) decomposes into a Fourier periodic block
plus a seq2seq model on the *residual* and unifies proactive and reactive through Model Predictive Control with chance
constraints: ≥36 % fewer SLO violations than proactive-only, reactive-only or loosely-coupled hybrids. DeepScaling [9]
(SoCC 2022) combines an ST-GNN forecast, a DNN CPU estimator and a DQN policy. AAPA [10] (2025) classifies workloads as
SPIKE / PERIODIC / RAMP / STATIONARY and applies a per-archetype strategy: up to 50 % fewer SLO violations and 40 %
lower latency than HPA, at **2–8× higher resource usage** under spike-dominated conditions. Kedify's KEDA predictive
scaler [11] uses Prophet, retrains every 6 hours (the cadence used here), gates on a configurable MAPE threshold and
returns a default when the model fails it — a shipped version of the validation gate on this project's roadmap.

Two patterns recur: the decision layer, not the forecaster, is where the engineering goes; and almost every reported
"predictive beats X" is measured against a reactive/threshold baseline or against other deep models, rarely against
same-time-yesterday. This project ran that harder comparison on its own synthetic scenarios — which is a property of
this harness, not a result borrowed from anything below.

## 2. What the evidence says about model choice for this series

The honest summary is that **model family matters less than the structure you remove before modelling, and simple
seasonal baselines are unusually strong on series of this shape.** **[Corrected 2026-09-22]** An earlier draft said
"at exactly this resolution". The cloud evidence below shares this system's 5–10-minute *sampling* resolution but not
its 10–60-minute *forecast horizon*; read it as suggestive of the regime, not as a measurement at our operating
point.

- **Cloud request-rate data specifically. [Corrected 2026-09-22]** Toner et al. [12] benchmarked six **zero-shot
  time-series foundation models** (TimesFM, Chronos-tiny, Moirai-small, TTM, VisionTS, Mamba4Cast) — pretrained
  models applied to these traces without any local fitting, *not* locally trained networks of the kind this
  repository deploys — against two simple baselines on four curated Huawei Cloud serverless request datasets (D1–D4),
  5-minute resolution, 8 640 steps, daily seasonality 288, on channels **selected** for high activity and discarded
  when "very low activity and/or highly random". Horizons were 30, 96 and 336 steps — **2.5, 8 and 28 hours**, one to
  two orders of magnitude beyond this system's 10–60 minutes. In the paper's per-dataset, per-horizon aggregate MASE
  table the naive seasonal forecaster beat every foundation model in all twelve dataset × horizon cells, with "a MASE
  typically half that of TimesFM"; the same holds for RMSSE. But the paper's *other* baseline, an online ridge-fitted
  linear model, **beat seasonal naive in four of those twelve cells** (D1/H30 1.404 vs 1.577; D2/H30 1.170 vs 1.231;
  D3/H30 1.082 vs 1.182; D3/H96 1.153 vs 1.179), so the paper does not show that copying yesterday is unbeatable —
  only that both cheap baselines beat the foundation models. Cloud series are strongly periodic with large spikes,
  which is the regime where a seasonal copy is hard to beat and where these models mis-handled the spikes.

  **What this review claims from it, and nothing more:** *our result is consistent with evidence that simple baselines
  can outperform zero-shot foundation models on selected cloud traces.* It is not evidence about locally trained
  LSTMs; it is not evidence at 10–60-minute horizons; it does not independently validate the comparisons in
  [`eval/RESULTS-2026-09-22-corrected.md`](../eval/RESULTS-2026-09-22-corrected.md), which stand or fall on their own
  run; and it does not excuse an implementation defect. A published finding that simple baselines are strong is not a
  reason to stop repairing a forecaster that is losing for reasons of its own.
- **Forecasting competitions.** M4 [13] found pure ML/NN methods worse than statistical methods and their combinations;
  the winner was Smyl's hybrid ES-RNN, whose neural part runs *after* exponential smoothing has removed per-series level
  and seasonality. M5 Accuracy [14] reversed the headline — all top methods were ML, LightGBM dominant, the best 22.4 %
  better than the best statistical benchmark — but the winners were **gradient-boosted trees on lag and calendar
  features**, not sequence networks; M5 Uncertainty [15] was won by per-quantile LightGBM over handcrafted features
  (rolling mean/median, day-of-week, skew/kurtosis, past quantiles). The transferable lesson is not "deep learning
  loses"; it is *tabular models on windowed lag + calendar features are the strongest cheap option, and deep models win
  only once the seasonal structure has been factored out.*
- **Direct benchmark contests.** Elsayed et al. [16] show a window-input GBRT matching or beating the DNNs on standard
  benchmarks; Zeng et al. [17] show a one-layer linear model (DLinear) beating transformer forecasters by 20–50 %. Both
  are **contested** — the DLinear result has been challenged on the grounds that size-matched transformers under the
  same protocol do better [18]. Treat "linear beats deep" as a claim about how these benchmarks are run, not a law.
- **Related-series pooling.** Montero-Manso and Hyndman [19] prove global (pooled) models are no more restrictive than
  per-series local models and have generalisation complexity that does not grow with the number of series — the
  principled route to a multi-tenant model and to cold start, and a better use of a neural network than one model per
  workload.
- **Retraining cadence.** Two 2025 studies of global-model retraining frequency [20][21] find that *reducing* retraining
  frequency preserves and sometimes improves both accuracy and forecast stability. A 6-hourly retrain on 7 days is more
  aggressive than the evidence requires, and frequent retraining with unseeded initialisation is a plausible contributor
  to the across-seed divergence documented in this repository.

## 3. Techniques this system does not use that the literature supports

**Quantile (pinball) forecasting instead of a point forecast under an asymmetric *squared* penalty. [Corrected
2026-09-22]** An earlier draft of this section said the deployed 2:1 penalty was "an indirect way of asking for the
0.667 quantile". **That is mathematically wrong and is withdrawn.** Which functional an asymmetric loss targets depends
on whether it is squared or absolute, and the two answers are different:

- **What the code does.** `ml-engine/models/lstm_model.py:83-104` (`asymmetric_mse`) computes
  `under_weight · step_weight · (y − ŷ)²`, with `under_weight = 2` when the forecast is low and `1` when it is high —
  asymmetric **squared** error. Ehm et al. [39] characterise the scoring functions consistent for the **expectile** at
  level α as exactly those of the form `|1(y<x) − α| · (φ(y) − φ(x) − φ′(x)(y − x))` with φ convex; the key case
  φ(t) = t² gives `(1−α)(x−y)²` for y < x and `α(x−y)²` for y ≥ x, which they identify as "the loss function used for
  estimation in expectile regression (Newey and Powell 1987; Efron 1991)", with α = ½ recovering ordinary least
  squares. A 2:1 under/over weighting sets α/(1−α) = 2, so the population optimum of the loss actually in the code is
  the **α = 2/3 expectile**.
- **What targets a quantile.** The 2/3 *quantile* is the optimum of asymmetric **absolute** (pinball, "linlin") loss —
  Ehm et al.'s [39] other family, `(1−α)(x−y)` for y < x and `α(y−x)` for y ≥ x, "at the heart of quantile regression"
  — which is the case Christoffersen and Diebold [22] state: under linlin loss with weights *a* on under-prediction
  and *b* on over-prediction, the optimal predictor is the conditional quantile at level *a/(a+b)*.

Expectiles are a coherent target in their own right — [39] gives them the same threshold-decision interpretation — but
they are **not** quantiles: they carry no coverage statement, and the 2/3 expectile is not the 2/3 quantile and does
not buy the same capacity margin. So the change worth testing is switching the objective to pinball loss at an
explicit τ, *and* being explicit about which functional is being targeted before and after. Quantile-forecasting
machinery is standard (DeepAR's sample-based quantiles [24]; LightGBM's quantile objective; per-quantile models as in
M5 Uncertainty [15]). It would also replace the hand-tuned 75th-percentile pattern rule with a learned, measurable
quantity.

**What τ may not be called. [Corrected 2026-09-22]** The newsvendor critical fractile `τ = c_u/(c_u + c_o)` [23] is
derived under *specified, linear* per-unit shortage and overage costs and an instantaneous stocking decision. None of
those conditions holds here: a pod costs a start-up delay before it serves, so shortage cost depends on *when* the
decision was taken; replicas are integers, so cost is a step function of the forecast rather than linear in it; the
scale-down stabilisation window makes a decision persist past the interval that motivated it; and neither `c_u` nor
`c_o` has been measured for this workload. Any starting τ — including the 0.8 in the shortlist below — is therefore a
**preselected hyperparameter to be tuned and reported**, not an economically derived quantity, and this document no
longer describes one as such.

**One change per experiment.** The forecasting objective (τ), the historical percentile used by the pattern component,
and the controller's confidence dampener all move the same effective capacity margin. Moving more than one at a time
makes the result uninterpretable, so each ablation changes one and holds the other two fixed.

**Conformal calibration of the capacity margin.** Rather than a hand-set confidence dampener, wrap any forecaster with
an online interval. Adaptive Conformal Inference [25] updates the nominal level α_t from realised coverage and holds
long-run coverage under distribution shift; EnbPI [26] builds intervals from leave-one-out ensemble residuals without a
calibration split. A 2026 benchmark of seven methods [27] found multi-step split CP with **horizon-specific
calibration** achieved target coverage with the tightest intervals (ACI close behind), while EnbPI and SPCI missed
nominal coverage — so prefer plain per-horizon residual-quantile calibration and *verify* coverage. Caveat from the same
paper: the guarantees weaken exactly when the residual process shifts, i.e. at level shifts.

**Direct multi-horizon rather than recursive.** The NN5 comparison of recursive, direct, DirRec, MIMO and DIRMO [28] and
the "rectify" work [29] establish that recursive strategies accumulate error across horizons while direct/MIMO do not.
Six-step direct output (the newest model here) is the right default; per-horizon calibration and per-horizon error
reporting should follow it.

**Feature sets the competition winners actually used [14][15]:** lags at 1, 2, 3, 6, 144 (24 h) and 1008 (7 d) steps;
rolling mean/median/max over 1 h, 6 h, 24 h; time-of-day and day-of-week as **Fourier pairs** rather than integer
indices (Prophet's decomposable model [30]); a weekend flag; event flags — here, deployment and release markers, the
observable cause of most level shifts.

**[Corrected 2026-09-22] — the 1008 lag is not free here.** At 10-minute resolution a lag of 1008 steps is **seven
days**, which is the entire rolling training window the deployed trainer uses: every row would need a further seven
days of history before that feature is defined, so with the current window the feature is undefined everywhere.
Either expand the history the trainer reads, or **omit lag 1008 initially** and keep {1, 2, 3, 6, 144}. The offline
selector experiment (`eval/selector.py`) takes the second option and says so in the code.

**Level shifts and regime change.** This repository's bias table (every history-based forecaster stuck 700–800 rpm low
after a level shift, only the trend-adaptive rule re-levelling) is the textbook failure. Three supported responses:
re-level by a bounded recent/last-week ratio (what `trend_adaptive` does); forecast the **residual** after subtracting a
current level or periodic estimate [8][13]; or detect the change and shrink the history window (ADWIN family [31]).

**Cold start.** The vendor answer is a minimum-history threshold plus a safe default: 24 h and forecast-only mode at AWS
[2], 7 days at Azure [3], fixed keep-alive fallback in Azure Functions [5], MAPE-threshold fallback at Kedify [11]. The
research answer is a pooled global model across workloads [19]. Foundation models are *not* a good cold-start answer
here on current evidence [12].

## 4. How these systems are evaluated

**Forecast-quality metrics, used carefully.** Rolling-origin evaluation is the standard and Tashman [32] is the
reference for its design choices (fixed vs rolling origin, updating vs recalibration, multiple test periods). Two
consequences for this harness: errors from **overlapping horizons are serially correlated**, so a naive paired test over
400 origins overstates significance — use HAC-corrected Diebold–Mariano, its multi-horizon extension [33], or a block
bootstrap; and MAPE misbehaves near zero, so report MASE [34] alongside per-horizon MAE (already the practice here). For
distributional forecasts use pinball / weighted quantile loss (ρ-risk), as MagicScaler [7] and DeepAR [24] do.

**Decision metrics, which are the ones that matter.** The systems literature does not score forecasts in the abstract:
Autopilot reports *slack* and OOM frequency [1]; OptScaler reports SLO violation rate as the **percentage of minutes in
violation**, the **accumulated magnitude** of violation, and average resource [8]; MagicScaler reports a
QoS-loss/resource-cost Pareto with a tolerance knob [7]; AAPA proposes a composite index and publishes its 2–8 ×
resource cost next to the SLO win [10]. The replica-minutes / minutes-short / scaling-events triple here is in the right
family; what is missing is one cost-weighted scalar of the Autopilot form — `w_u · under-provisioned pod-minutes +
w_o · over-provisioned pod-minutes + w_Δ · scaling events` — with the same weights used to *train* (via τ) and to
*score*. Without it, arms cannot be ranked when one is cheaper and another is short less often. Any such scalar built
from forecast error alone is a **proxy** for operating cost, not operating cost: it prices neither start-up delay nor
the integer replica step nor a dropped request. Say so wherever it is reported.

**Measure offered demand, not served demand.** The quantity forecast and scored must be the load *arriving*, not the
load the system managed to serve. If the metric is served throughput, then an overloaded, shedding system reports
falling demand, the forecaster learns the drop, and the autoscaler scales *down* into an incident. The offline harness
is safe by construction — its scenario series is offered demand and `replica_need()` is deliberately unconstrained by
the replica ceiling, with ceiling-limited minutes reported separately — but the same property has to be preserved in
whatever metric the deployed forecaster reads.

## 5. Ranked shortlist

Ranked by expected value per unit of effort. Each is testable in the existing harness (`eval/offline_eval.py`: five
scenario families × two data seeds, 400 rolling origins, real Go controller replay).

**1. Per-workload selection between the arithmetic forecasters (Autopilot-inspired).** *Change:* keep
`seasonal_pattern` and `trend_adaptive` as an ensemble, add a cheap lagged linear arm, and at each origin serve the
candidate with the lowest exponentially-smoothed cost on **matured** targets, plus a switching penalty in the same
cost units (the shape of Autopilot Eq. 8–9 [1]). *Why:* it is what this repository's own results point at — the best
arm flips with the workload — and Autopilot ships this mechanism in production. **[Corrected 2026-09-22]** No number
is borrowed from Autopilot for it: the 31 % → 23 % slack difference is a cohort comparison, not a selector-only
ablation (§1), and its candidates are resource-limit recommenders rather than forecasts, so the gain available *here*
is **unknown until measured here**. AAPA [10] is the same idea as archetype conditioning. *Test:* the offline
experiment in `eval/selector.py`, on test periods and seeds untouched by parameter selection, against **each** fixed
arm and a constant blend, with block-bootstrap uncertainty over whole days. *Adopt if:* it clears the bar predeclared
in [`eval/SELECTOR-RESULTS-2026-09-22.md`](../eval/SELECTOR-RESULTS-2026-09-22.md) — a practical pooled cost-proxy
improvement whose interval excludes zero, non-inferiority on the repeating workload, and no unacceptable increase in
shortage, failure or switching. Hindsight per-scenario winners are a **diagnostic**, not a selectable baseline, and
overlapping rolling origins are not independent samples. *Effort:* days.

**2. Direct per-step quantile model on lag/calendar features, replacing point forecast + asymmetric MSE.** *Change:*
six LightGBM (or linear quantile regression) models, one per horizon, trained with **pinball** loss at an explicit τ —
**[Corrected 2026-09-22]** starting at τ = 0.8 as a *preselected hyperparameter to be tuned and reported*, **not** as
a newsvendor-derived quantity (see §3 for why the critical-fractile derivation does not apply here) — on lags
{1, 2, 3, 6, 144} (**omit 1008 until the history window is longer than seven days**), rolling mean/median/max over
1 h/6 h/24 h, and Fourier time-of-day and day-of-week terms. *Why:* [39] and [22] settle which functional each
asymmetric loss targets — the loss in the code today targets an **expectile**, not a quantile, so this is a change of
target and not only of optimiser; [14][15][16] are the evidence that GBMs on windowed lag features are the strongest
cheap family at this resolution.
*Test:* pinball loss at τ (primary), MAE and per-step bias (secondary), then controller replay. *Adopt if:* it beats
`seasonal_pattern` on pinball at τ on ≥4 of 5 scenarios and loses no more than 5 % of MAE. *Effort:* 1–2 weeks. This is
also the honest replacement for the neural component if that is removed.

**3. Online conformal margin instead of the fixed 75th percentile and the confidence dampener.** *Change:* keep
per-horizon residual quantiles over the last K matured origins, serve `forecast + margin_h`, and update the nominal
level with the ACI recursion [25]. *Why:* it turns the safety margin into a measured coverage target, and
horizon-specific calibration was the best performer in the recent benchmark [27]. *Test:* per-horizon empirical
coverage, interval width, minutes-short at matched replica-minutes. *Adopt if:* coverage within ±2 points of target on
all five scenarios **and** the same shortfall bought for fewer replica-minutes. *Effort:* ~1 week.

**4. Explicit re-levelling / residual forecasting for level shifts.** *Change:* subtract a current level estimate
(robust recent median, or a Fourier-periodic fit as in OptScaler [8]) before forecasting and add it back; optionally
shrink the history window on an ADWIN-style alarm [31]. *Why:* the levelshift bias table shows every history-based arm
stuck ~700 rpm low across the whole horizon, and the literature's answer is decomposition and window shrinkage, not a
bigger model. *Test:* levelshift and weekly scenarios, signed bias per horizon. *Adopt if:* levelshift bias falls >50 %
at +30 min with <3 % MAE loss on `repeating`. *Effort:* ~1 week.

**5. Cut retraining to daily and add a publication gate.** *Change:* retrain every 24 h; before publishing, score the
candidate against the incumbent *and* the seasonal baseline on the most recent held-out origins and publish only on a
win. *Why:* [20][21] find less frequent retraining preserves or improves accuracy and stability; Kedify ships the
threshold gate [11]; it also attacks the across-seed divergence and the "stale model overwrites a good one" failure.
*Test:* rerun the sweep at 24 h cadence; compare MAE and its variance across model seeds. *Adopt if:* no scenario loses
more than 3 % MAE. *Effort:* days — lowest risk on this list.

**6. If the network is kept: deseasonalise before it, seed it, and make it an arm, not the default.** *Change:* train
on the residual after removing the seasonal component (ES-RNN [13] / OptScaler [8] structure) with seeded
initialisation, per-window normalisation and gradient clipping, one change per experiment as the roadmap specifies.
*Why:* every competition-winning neural result cited here sits on a deseasonalising layer; a plain sequence model on
raw levels is the configuration the evidence most consistently rejects [12][13][17]. *Test:* the preregistered 10 % bar
against the strongest baseline across ≥3 model seeds per run. *Adopt if:* it clears the bar on ≥3 of 5 scenarios with
seed variance under 10 % of the mean; otherwise ship it off by default and say so.

*Not recommended:* zero-shot foundation models. **[Corrected 2026-09-22]** On four curated Huawei Cloud request
datasets the naive seasonal forecaster beat all six tested zero-shot models in every reported dataset × horizon cell,
and an online linear model beat them too [12]. Those horizons were 2.5–28 hours and those traces were selected, so
this is a reason not to *start* with a foundation model on series of this kind — not a measurement at our operating
point, and not a finding about locally trained models.

## 6. Where the evidence is weak or contested

- **DLinear vs transformers [17][18]** is an open dispute about protocol, not a settled result. "Linear beats deep" is
  not established.
- **MagicScaler's forecasting baselines [7]** are three transformers and a GP — ARIMA and RNN/CNN were excluded by
  reference to another paper, and no seasonal-naive baseline appears — so its forecast win is a win over deep models
  only. Its *scaling* comparison is more informative and less flattering: a past-week statistics-plus-buffer policy,
  essentially this repository's seasonal pattern, was substantially cheaper in resource cost.
- **AAPA [10]** is simulation over weakly-labelled traces, and its SLO win comes with 2–8× resource usage.
- **The Prophet+LSTM Kubernetes study [35]** reports 65–90 % improvement over single models, but compares only against
  ARIMA/LSTM/Bi-LSTM, not same-time-yesterday, and says the Kubernetes integration is conceptual. Not evidence that a
  hybrid beats a seasonal baseline.
- **The "safety net" chapter [36]** (ARIMA/XGBoost/LSTM at one-minute resolution, XGBoost reported strongest) was
  available here only as publisher metadata and a secondary summary; verify before relying on its numbers.
- **Kedify [11]** is a vendor write-up: useful for operational shape, not comparative accuracy.
- **Autopilot [1], Serverless in the Wild [5], AHPA [6], OptScaler [8]** are the strongest sources — production
  deployments with described mechanisms and stated cost metrics. Where this review is confident, it rests on them
  plus [12].
- **[Corrected 2026-09-22] Autopilot's published slack figures are observational.** §4.2 compares cohorts of jobs
  that happened to use different recommenders, and the paper flags the sampling bias itself. Take the *mechanism*
  (Eq. 8–9) from Autopilot and measure the effect locally; do not import the effect size.
- **[Corrected 2026-09-22] Toner et al. [12] is about zero-shot models at long horizons.** Its subjects are
  pretrained foundation models applied without local fitting, at 2.5–28 hours, on channels selected for
  forecastability. It supports caution about that family on cloud data. It says nothing about a locally trained
  network at a 60-minute horizon, and it is not corroboration for this project's own offline comparisons.
